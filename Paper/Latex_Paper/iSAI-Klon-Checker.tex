\begin{abstract}
In Thai \thgloss{Klon-Paed}{กลอนแปด} poetry (the eight-syllable verse form) each four-line stanza must satisfy an interlocking rhyming pattern
where each rhyming syllables must share both a vowel sound \thpar{sara}{สระ} and a final-consonant
class \thpar{mattra}{มาตราตัวสะกด}. Because Thai is written without word boundaries and its
orthography alters form or conceals implicit vowels, silenced letters, and consonant clusters.
Automatic verification of rhymes is non-trivial, and neither classical rule-based checkers nor
grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P) romanization checkers deal with the resulting edge cases reliably.
We present a hybrid rule-based verification approach.
First, we improve the KhaveeVerifier algorithm---vowel analysis,
final-consonant-class analysis, the rhyme test, karun silencing, and true-final
detection---and ship it in PyThaiNLP 5.3.5 (Model~B). Second, we introduce
Klonpad (Model~D), which augments these rules with a gold-standard
G2P override dictionary of 4{,}292 entries and a fallback segmenter,
recovering phonetic blind spots of the base oracle. We evaluate five checker
configurations on a gold corpus of 36{,}475 classical stanzas (145{,}709 rhyme
checks) and on a targeted augmentation of 11{,}623 instances that includes
10{,}000 adversarial negatives and 423 oracle-blind rhymes. The best
configuration reaches 88.5\% gold recall and 86.9\% F1 on the augmentation
while maintaining 100\% precision on curated negatives and recovering 95.7\%
of the oracle-blind rhymes. The verifier serves both as an explainable,
zero-hallucination educational tool and as a deterministic reward model for
reinforcement-learning-based poetry generation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Thai orthography does not map straightforwardly onto pronunciation, and
this gap becomes especially apparent in the classical register used by Thai
poetry. The script is written without explicit word boundaries; vowels are
frequently implicit; consonants or entire clusters can be silenced by the
\thgloss{thanthakhat}{ทัณฑฆาต} (karun) diacritic; and irregular words whose pronunciations do
not follow the standard phonetic rules are common. Complex phonological
structures, such as consonant blending \thpar{\emph{kham khuap klam}}{คำควบกล้ำ} and leading
consonants \thpar{\emph{aksorn nam}}{อักษรนำ}, further obscure syllable boundaries, vowel
quality, and tone in an already unsegmented writing system. As a result,
syllabification, pronunciation, and tone assignment are genuinely difficult
tasks for both human learners and computational systems.

Klon-Paed, the eight-syllable Thai verse form, depends on exactly these
phonetic properties. Each stanza \thpar{\emph{bot}}{บท} consists of four
sets of seven-to-nine-syllable lines \thpar{\emph{wak}}{วรรค}. A valid rhyme between two syllables
requires that they share both the vowel sound \thpar{sara}{สระ} and the
final-consonant class \thpar{mattra}{มาตราตัวสะกด}. Four canonical rhyme rules must hold within (r1, r2, and r3) and across stanzas (rX)
(Section~\ref{sec:bg}), and the final syllable of every wak participates in
the scheme. A single silent letter---for example the silent \emph{r} of
\emph{\thgloss{phhet}{เพชร}} (`diamond')---or a misread vowel length is enough to break a
rhyme. These constraints are strict and deterministic, which is precisely
what makes them hard for statistical systems.

Two communities need a reliable automatic verifier for Klon-Paed. In
\emph{education}, teachers and students need feedback that is
deterministic, explainable, and free of hallucination: an LLM that
confidently invents a rhyme that does not exist is worse than useless in a
classroom. In \emph{NLP research}, training a language model to compose
Klon-Paed, for instance through reinforcement learning (RL), requires a
programmatic reward signal that scores rhyme correctness exactly. Because
large language models (LLMs) are probabilistic engines, they are
intrinsically unreliable at strict constraints such as syllable counts and
rhymes; a rule-based verifier supplies the ground truth that such
generative pipelines require.

Existing verification systems fall short of this standard. The historical
KhaveeVerifier in PyThaiNLP 5.0.1 (Model~A) relies on dictionary subword
tokenisation, omits one of the four canonical rhyme rules, and reaches only
73.4\% recall on our gold corpus. Grapheme-to-phoneme (G2P)
romanisation-based checkers, such as the \texttt{word\_check} grader of the
KlonSuphap-LM generator~\cite{klonsuphap} (Model~C), collapse long/short
vowel distinctions and produce thousands of false positives on adversarial
negatives. Neither family handles the phonetic blind spots of Thai
orthography in a principled way. In this paper we address these
limitations with a hybrid rule-based approach and a rigorous, adversarial
evaluation. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An improved rule-based verifier (Model~B).} We rework the
vowel analysis (\texttt{check\_sara}), the final-consonant-class analysis
(\texttt{check\_marttra}), the rhyme test (\texttt{is\_sumpus}), karun
silencing, and true-final detection, and implement all four canonical rhyme
rules. The result is merged into PyThaiNLP 5.3.5 as the default
\texttt{KhaveeVerifier}.
\item \textbf{A dictionary-enhanced variant (Klonpad, Model~D).} We augment
the rules with a gold-standard 4{,}292-entry G2P override dictionary and a
fallback segmenter, recovering the oracle's blind spots.
\item \textbf{A large evaluation corpus.} 36{,}475 gold stanzas (145{,}709
rhyme checks) from five classical Thai works, plus 11{,}623 targeted
augmentation instances---10{,}000 oracle-verified negatives, 1{,}200 tricky
positives, and 423 oracle-blind positives---each audited in independent
review passes.
\item \textbf{A systematic comparison.} Five configurations (A, B, C,
D\_w2p, D\_ssg) are compared on precision, recall, F1, coverage, and
confidence intervals, with per-rule and per-operator error analyses.
\item \textbf{Two deployments.} An explainable educational web tool and a
deterministic reward environment for RL-based Klon-Paed generation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{The Linguistic Rules of Klon-Paed}

A Klon-Paed stanza has four waks of eight syllables each. Thai syllables
are written without delimiters and a word may consist of one or more
syllables, so the metre must be recovered by syllabification rather than by
counting words. Within this skeleton the verse obeys a fixed rhyme scheme.
Two syllables rhyme \thpar{\emph{sumpus}}{สัมผัส} when they share the same vowel sound
\thpar{sara}{สระ} and the same final-consonant class \thpar{mattra}{มาตราตัวสะกด}, as defined by the Royal
Institute of Thailand; tone is not part of the rhyme. The mattra classes
\thpar{\emph{mae}}{แม่}---ka, kong, kok, kot, kon, kop, kom, koey, and koewa---group
the final consonants of Thai syllables by their phonetic outcome (the
velar, dental, labial, nasal, semivowel, and w finals). The four waks are
the \thpar{\emph{sadap}}{สดับ}, \thpar{\emph{rap}}{รับ}, \thpar{\emph{rong}}{รอง}, and
\thpar{\emph{song}}{ส่ง}. Four canonical
rhyme rules are checked in this work, following the \emph{klon} canon and
the implementation of the PyThaiNLP \texttt{KhaveeVerifier}:
\begin{itemize}
\item r1 (\emph{sadap} $\rightarrow$ \emph{rap}): the final syllable of
wak~1 must rhyme with one of the first five syllables of wak~2 (syllables
3 and 5 are obligatory, 1, 2, and 4 are permissible);
\item r2 (\emph{rap} $\rightarrow$ \emph{rong}): the final syllable of
wak~2 must rhyme with the final syllable of wak~3;
\item r3 (\emph{rong} $\rightarrow$ \emph{song}): the final syllable of
wak~3 must rhyme with one of the first five syllables of wak~4;
\item rX (inter-stanza): the final syllable of wak~4 of the previous
stanza must rhyme with the final syllable of wak~2 of the current stanza.
\end{itemize}
As a concrete example, the well-known stanza of the
\thgloss{\emph{Phukao Thong}}{นิราศภูเขาทอง} travel poem by Sunthorn Phu
satisfies the intra-stanza rules r1--r3:

Three orthographic phenomena make these rules hard to apply automatically.
First, compound, transformed, and reduced vowels \thpar{\emph{sara prasom}}{สระประสม},
\thpar{\emph{sara plianrup}}{สระเปลี่ยนรูป}, \thpar{\emph{sara lotrup}}{สระลดรูป} let several spellings map to one
vowel sound. Second, the thanthakhat (karun) diacritic silences a letter
or a whole cluster, so the written form contains letters that are not
pronounced. Third, a trailing \emph{y}, \emph{l}, or \emph{w} may be a
genuine final consonant or merely part of a vowel digraph or an initial
cluster. Any verifier must resolve all three before it can compare vowel
and final class.

\subsection{Thai Text-Processing Foundations}

Two building blocks underlie all Thai NLP systems: segmentation and
grapheme-to-phoneme conversion. Because Thai has no word delimiters,
segmentation has received sustained attention: dictionary-based maximum
matching (the \texttt{newmm} engine of PyThaiNLP~\cite{pythainlp}),
neural segmenters such as DeepCut~\cite{deepcut} and
AttaCut~\cite{attacut}, syllable-based neural
segmentation~\cite{chormai2020syllable}, and the SSG syllable segmenter
shipped in PyThaiNLP~5.x, which the present work uses throughout. G2P
conversion maps surface orthography to pronunciation; Thai G2P has been
addressed with rule-based systems such as TLTK~\cite{tltk},
example-based methods~\cite{charoenpornsawat2006}, CRF-based joint-sequence
models~\cite{saychum2016}, and two-stage sequence
models~\cite{rugchatjaroen2019}. G2P is directly relevant to rhyme
verification because rhyme is a property of \emph{sound}, not of spelling:
Model~C in this study performs its comparison in the romanised space
produced by the TLTK G2P model.

\subsection{Computational Poetry Analysis and Generation}

Prior work on Thai poetry in NLP has concentrated on analysis and
generation. On the analysis side, machine-learning studies of Sunthorn
Phu's verse have extracted sound patterns---rhyme, assonance, and
alliteration---using internal-rhyme
rules~\cite{suksanguan2021}. On the generation side, the dominant line of
work fine-tunes language models on corpora of classical verse: the Phra
Aphai Mani LM~\cite{phraaphai} and its successor KlonSuphap-
LM~\cite{klonsuphap} fine-tune a GPT-2 Thai base model, then use
\emph{rhyme tagging} (annotating the tokens that must rhyme), attention
masking, and finally reinforcement learning in which a rule-based
\emph{klon grader} supplies the reward. That grader is the
\texttt{word\_check} module evaluated here as Model~C, making our
evaluation directly relevant to the RL step of that pipeline. More broadly,
RLHF has established reward models as essential for aligning LLMs to
follow strict requirements~\cite{ouyang2022}, and grammar-constrained
decoding has been proposed to enforce structural constraints at inference
time~\cite{geng2023}. For classical Thai specifically, recent benchmarks
report that even strong LLMs struggle with archaic vocabulary and verse
structure~\cite{triyason2026}, which motivates dedicated deterministic
tools.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

Compared with this landscape, our contribution is the first systematic,
adversarially augmented evaluation of Thai rhyme \emph{verification} as
opposed to generation. The historical rule-based checker (Model~A) has
never been stress-tested against curated edge cases; the G2P-based grader
of KlonSuphap-LM (Model~C) has not been validated for precision; and no
open verifier covers all four rhyme rules while providing dictionary
support for irregular pronunciations. We fill this gap with improved
rule-based verification (Model~B), a dictionary-enhanced variant
(Model~D), and a large gold-plus-augmentation evaluation that quantifies
precision and recall, including a novel \emph{oracle-blind} probe that
holds the verifier itself accountable for rhymes its own oracle cannot
see.

\section{Methodology: Hybrid Verification Architecture}

\subsection{Core Rule-Based Verification (Model B)}

Model~B is the \texttt{KhaveeVerifier} of PyThaiNLP 5.3.5. It verifies a
poem in three stages. (1)~Each wak is tokenised into syllables with the
SSG segmenter. (2)~For each canonical rule the relevant syllables are
extracted: the final syllable of every wak, and the first five syllables of
waks~2 and~4. (3)~The rhyme test \texttt{is\_sumpus} decides whether two
syllables rhyme; a rule holds if the test succeeds for at least one
permitted target. The eight-syllable metre is additionally reported.

The accuracy of the verifier rests on three functions.
\texttt{check\_sara} returns the canonical vowel of a syllable. It handles
simple vowels, compound vowels (\emph{sara prasom}), transformed and
reduced vowels (\emph{sara plianrup/lotrup}), the special vowels
\thpar{\emph{sara koen}}{สระเกิน}: \emph{am}, \emph{ai}, \emph{ao}), Pali/Sanskrit words
with silent terminal vowels, and words whose karun-stripped form changes
the vowel. \texttt{check\_marttra} returns the Royal-Institute
final-consonant class of a syllable, strictly by orthography. It resolves
karun first, strips a silent \emph{r} in the \emph{tr}/\emph{chr}/\emph{pr}
clusters of loanwords (e.g., \emph{but}, \emph{net}, \thgloss{\emph{phhet}}{เพชร}),
handles single-character words, and classifies the \emph{sara koen}
orthographically as \emph{mae ka}; phonetic rhyming for these is restored
later in \texttt{is\_sumpus}. Finally, \texttt{is\_sumpus} applies phonetic
normalisation for the \emph{sara koen}: \emph{a+koey} $\rightarrow$
\emph{ai+ka} (so \emph{chai} rhymes with \emph{sai}) and
\emph{a/am+kom} $\rightarrow$ \emph{am+ka} (so \emph{cham} rhymes with
\emph{kam}). It returns true iff the normalised sara and mattra of the two
syllables are equal.

Two further mechanisms account for most of the improvement over the 5.0.1
baseline. \texttt{handle\_karun\_sound\_silence} strips characters silenced
by the thanthakhat (e.g., \thgloss{\emph{ohm}}{โอห์ม} $\rightarrow$ \emph{o}), so that
\emph{ohm} and \emph{o} are judged to rhyme. \texttt{\_is\_true\_final}
decides whether a trailing \emph{y}, \emph{l}, or \emph{w} is a genuine
final consonant rather than part of a vowel digraph or an initial cluster:
the \emph{y} of \emph{thai} is silent, the \emph{l} of \emph{plae} belongs
to the initial cluster, but the \emph{l} of \emph{chon} is a true final.

Compared with the 5.0.1 verifier (Model~A), the changes are: (i)~SSG
syllable segmentation replaces dictionary subword tokenisation, which
missegments compounds and fails on out-of-vocabulary words; (ii)~karun
silencing and true-final detection are implemented; (iii)~phonetic
normalisation in \texttt{is\_sumpus} is applied to both words independently
(the 5.0.1 \texttt{elif} chain normalises at most one word, so rhymes in
which both words belong to the same special-vowel class can be missed);
(iv)~the r3 rule is implemented (5.0.1 has no r3); and (v)~single-character
tokens no longer crash the mattra analysis.

\subsection{Dictionary-Enhanced Verification (Klonpad, Model D)}

Model~B is linguistically principled, but it inherits a blind spot from
its own design: \texttt{check\_sara} returns only the first vowel of a
token, so a multi-syllable word that SSG keeps as one token loses the vowel
of its final (rhyming) syllable, and the silent-\emph{r} loanwords are
stripped only in a whitelisted subset of clusters. Klonpad (Model~D)
addresses this by layering a gold-standard pronunciation dictionary on top
of Model~B's rules.

The dictionary, \texttt{POETRY\_OVERRIDES}, contains 4{,}292 hand-verified
entries mapping a surface form to its syllable sequence (e.g.,
\thgloss{\emph{phhet}}{เพชร} $\rightarrow$ \emph{phet}; \thgloss{\emph{sattruu}}{ศัตรู} $\rightarrow$
\emph{sat-truu}; \thgloss{\emph{kasatrii}}{กษัตรี} $\rightarrow$ \emph{ka-sat-trii}). It was
built from the frequent vocabulary of the Phra Aphai Mani corpus with
manual supervision, and every entry is checked by a four-guard pipeline
that rejects pronunciations hallucinated by the Word2Phrase (w2p) neural
G2P model; w2p-derived candidates are kept only as labelled hints and are
never trusted on their own. At inference time each syllable is first looked
up in the override dictionary; if absent, a fallback segmenter supplies the
pronunciation. We report two configurations: \texttt{D\_w2p}, which falls
back to the w2p neural G2P model, and \texttt{D\_ssg}, which falls back to
plain SSG segmentation without w2p. As Section~\ref{sec:results} shows,
the w2p fallback introduces pronunciation hallucinations on short or
compound words, and \texttt{D\_ssg} is the more robust configuration.

\subsection{Baseline Systems}

Model~A is the original \texttt{KhaveeVerifier} of PyThaiNLP 5.0.1,
instrumented verbatim from the vendored source: dictionary-based
\texttt{subword\_tokenize}, no r3 rule, no karun handling, and no
true-final logic. Model~C is the \texttt{word\_check} rhyme grader of the
KlonSuphap-LM project~\cite{klonsuphap}: each wak is romanised with the
TLTK G2P model and the romanised vowels and finals are compared. Its two
documented weaknesses are (i)~a \texttt{replace\_long\_short} step that
collapses long and short vowels, and (ii)~a mattra comparison that ignores
the final consonant; both inflate recall at the cost of precision, and
tltk failures reduce coverage.

\section{Dataset Construction and Augmentation}

\subsection{Gold-Standard Corpus Compilation}

We collected five classical Thai narrative poems from the Vajirayana
digital library~\cite{vajirayana}: \thgloss{\emph{Khobut}}{โคบุตร},
\thgloss{\emph{Khun Chang Khun Phaen}}{ขุนช้างขุนแผน}, \thgloss{\emph{Phra Aphai Mani}}{พระอภัยมณี},
\thgloss{\emph{Phukao Thong}}{ภูเขาทอง}, and \thgloss{\emph{Suphasaet Son Ying}}{สุภาษิตสอนหญิง}. A cleaning pipeline normalised the text and split it into
stanzas of four waks, discarding only chapter-opening fragments and
scribal \emph{o} marks. Crucially, waks with ten or more syllables were
retained rather than dropped: removing them silently severs the
inter-stanza rhyme chains and made an early version of the corpus appear to
violate rX at a ~72\% rate. On the complete corpus rX holds at ~96--97\%
for the strongest checkers, confirming that the fix was correct. The final
corpus comprises 191 files, 36{,}475 stanzas, 145{,}900 waks, and 145{,}709
gold rhyme checks (Table~\ref{tab:corpus}); the gold is all-positive,
because the classical poems are presumed to rhyme. An alternative public
corpus covering similar material is the Thai Literature
Corpora~\cite{tlc}.

\subsection{Targeted Data Augmentation}

Recall over an all-positive corpus cannot measure precision, and easy
positives cannot expose blind spots. We therefore augmented the gold with
adversarial instances generated by an automated pipeline. Every instance
is \emph{standalone}: it is derived from a real stanza but only the target
rule is changed, so exactly one rule is exercised while the others remain
intact. Every negative is verified by the 5.3.5 oracle
(\texttt{is\_sumpus}) to be a genuine non-rhyme, and the whole set was
audited in four independent passes in which reviewers spelled out the sara
and mattra of every candidate (with vowel length made explicit) and
double-checked pronunciations against the IPA. The audit removed 56 words
from the candidate pool that the oracle misreads---karan clusters it fails
to silence, and multi-syllable Pali words SSG keeps as single tokens.

\paragraph{Negatives (precision probes).} The 10{,}000 negatives are drawn
from eight corruption operators (Table~\ref{tab:augmix}): trivial
different-vowel-and-mattra swaps (C6), same-mattra swaps (C0), same-vowel
swaps (C1), short/long vowel swaps (C3), liquid-final swaps (C4), a
systematic trap in which the 5.0.1 checker reads the candidate as rhyming
with the target while 5.3.5 does not (C9), leading-consonant words (C5),
and karun/\thgloss{\emph{ru}}{ฤ}/cluster traps (C2). C3 and C9 dominate the mix because
they are the operators that best discriminate the checkers.

\paragraph{Positives (recall probes).} The 1{,}200 \emph{tricky}
positives swap the target syllable with a rhyming candidate that exercises
normalisation \thpar{\emph{sara plianrup/lotrup}}{สระเปลี่ยนรูป/ลดรูป}, \thgloss{\emph{ru}}{ฤ}, karun, and
true-final cases; all are oracle-confirmed rhymes. A further 423
\emph{oracle-blind} positives are genuine rhymes that the oracle itself
cannot verify and therefore labels as non-rhymes: the silent-\emph{r}
\thgloss{\emph{phhet}}{เพชร} (true \emph{eh}+kot, which the oracle reads with the long
\emph{ae} because the dead final carries no short-vowel mark), and the
first-sara words \thgloss{\emph{sattruu}}{ศัตรู}, \thgloss{\emph{kasatrii}}{กษัตรี}, and \thgloss{\emph{kasatriiya}}{กษัตรีย์}
(SSG keeps each as one token, so \texttt{check\_sara} hears only the first
vowel). For these instances the gold label is the \emph{linguistic} truth,
not the oracle verdict---a checker that hears the real rhyme receives
credit, while the oracle itself (Model~B) is charged a false negative.
This is the quantitative statement of ``the oracle that is wrong loses
points.''

\section{Evaluation and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We report precision, recall, and F1 with Wilson 95\% confidence intervals,
coverage (the share of units evaluated), and a conservative drop-as-fail
variant for Model~C. Because every augmentation instance is standalone and
tests exactly one rule, the per-rule prediction is the clean metric; a
stanza-level aggregate over the augmentation is only well-defined for
Model~B (which is the oracle). Model~A has no r3 and is reported as N/A
there. All checkers were instrumented from verbatim vendored sources (the
5.0.1 and 5.3.5 cores), with Model~C run under Python 3.12 through a
persistent pool of ten tltk workers (the full augmentation pass takes about
40 minutes; A/B/D are parallelised and finish in under a minute). Every
number in this section is recomputed from the committed result files.

\subsection{Gold-Corpus Recall}

Table~\ref{tab:gold} reports stanza-level and per-rule recall over the
36{,}475 gold stanzas. The proposed family dominates the baseline:
D\_ssg reaches 88.5\% stanza recall and B 87.5\%, versus 73.4\% for
Model~A. Model~C reaches 84.8\% but evaluates only 95.7\% of the corpus
(tltk failures concentrate in the archaic Khun Chang Khun Phaen); its
figures use the coverage-corrected denominator.

\subsection{Augmentation: Precision, Recall, and F1}

Table~\ref{tab:aug} summarises the augmentation-only results (pooled per
rule) and Table~\ref{tab:augrules} breaks them down per rule. Three
results stand out.

First, Model~B is a perfect oracle on the negatives: it makes zero false
positives across all 10{,}000 curated negatives (100\% precision by
construction, since the negatives were oracle-verified). Its recall is
limited only by the oracle-blind positives it cannot see (73.9\%).

Second, Model~D recovers most of those blind spots: D\_w2p reaches 92.1\%
recall and D\_ssg 87.0\%, with precision 83.4\% and 86.8\% respectively,
giving the best F1 on the augmentation (87.5\% for D\_w2p). The
oracle-blind column of Table~\ref{tab:ob} shows the mechanism: D\_w2p
hears 405 of the 423 oracle-blind rhymes (95.7\%), D\_ssg 288 (68.1\%),
whereas B hears none.

Third, the baselines invert the precision/recall trade-off. Model~C is
recall-strong (87.1\% on the augmentation, 78.7\% even on oracle-blind
rhymes, because its G2P romanisation hears the real sound) but makes
3{,}107 false positives---2{,}702 of them on the short/long vowel swap
(C3), which its \texttt{replace\_long\_short} collapses---for an
augmentation precision of only 30.4\%. Model~A is weak on both sides
(28.4\% precision, 62.7\% recall), with 1{,}774 of its 1{,}950 false
positives coming from the C9 operator (words the 5.0.1
\texttt{is\_sumpus} reads with the same sara/mattra as the target while
5.3.5 splits them).

\subsection{Discussion}

The experiment supports four findings. (1)~\textbf{The proposed rules are
precise.} B never false-accepts on oracle-verified negatives, and its
precision transfers to the merged gold+augmentation setting, where B,
D\_w2p, and D\_ssg all exceed 99.5\% precision while A and C stay at 95\%
and 92\%. (2)~\textbf{The override dictionary recovers the oracle's blind
spots.} The D\_w2p-versus-D\_ssg gap on oracle-blind recall (95.7\% versus
68.1\%) is a clean, mechanistic demonstration of the pronunciation-knowledge
contribution: the curated overrides and w2p both read \emph{phhet} as
/phet/ and split \emph{sattruu} and \emph{kasatrii} into their true
syllables. (3)~\textbf{C trades precision for recall on exactly the
vowel-length distinction} that the rule-based family enforces; its G2P
romanisation is useful for hearing real rhymes (78.7\% oracle-blind) but
its length collapse makes it unusable as a precision grader.
(4)~\textbf{The oracle itself has a blind spot that only the oracle-blind
probe exposes.} A purely oracle-supervised evaluation would have reported
B as flawless, hiding the deduction that our probe applies.

Overall, D\_ssg is the best configuration on the gold corpus (88.5\%
recall) and D\_w2p the best on the augmentation (87.5\% F1). On the merged
gold+augmentation set, the F1 favours D\_ssg (93.4\%), followed by D\_w2p
(92.9\%) and B (92.8\%), with C (88.0\%) and A (82.3\%) behind; the merged
precision is gold-dominated (all checkers $\geq$ 92\%), so the precision
that discriminates the systems lives in the augmentation-only tables.

\section{Applications and Future Work}

\subsection{Educational Impact}

The verifier is deployed as an interactive web tool for teachers and
students of Klon 4/8. The interface accepts a poem, segments it into
syllables, and displays each wak with its rhyme positions highlighted and
the sara and mattra of every syllable spelled out. Errors are reported
deterministically and explained in terms of the four rhyme rules, so a
student can see exactly why a line fails (for example, a short/long vowel
mismatch or a misread karun). Because the system is rule-based, its
feedback is reproducible and free of hallucination---properties that
general-purpose LLMs cannot guarantee. Future work includes classroom
evaluations of the tool and pronunciation guidance for irregular words.

\subsection{Foundation for Generative AI}

The same determinism makes the verifier an ideal reward model for training
small, locally run LLMs to compose Klon-Paed. Reinforcement learning
requires a dense, consistent reward signal; the verifier provides one that
is exact, immediate, and free of the noise of a learned reward model. This
mirrors the RL step of the KlonSuphap-LM pipeline~\cite{klonsuphap}, but
with a grader (our Model~B or~D) that our evaluation shows to be
substantially more precise than the G2P-based grader used there
(Model~C), particularly on vowel length. Future work includes scaling the
override dictionary to more corpora, adding context-sensitive
pronunciations for heteronyms, and integrating the verifier into a
decoding-time constrained sampler for poetry
generation~\cite{geng2023}.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a hybrid rule-based approach to Thai Klon-Paed rhyme
verification. An improved rule-based verifier (Model~B), now the default
\texttt{KhaveeVerifier} of PyThaiNLP 5.3.5, implements all four canonical
rhyme rules with robust handling of karun, true finals, and sara
normalisation, and reaches 100\% precision on curated negatives. A
dictionary-enhanced variant (Klonpad, Model~D) adds a gold-standard
4{,}292-entry G2P override dictionary and recovers 95.7\% of the oracle's
own blind spots. Evaluated on 36{,}475 gold stanzas and 11{,}623
adversarial instances against two external baselines, the proposed family
raises stanza recall from 73.4\% (PyThaiNLP 5.0.1) to 88.5\% (D\_ssg)
while maintaining near-perfect precision, and exposes---quantitatively---the
blind spots of both the G2P-romanisation checker and the rule-based oracle
itself. The verifier is released as an explainable educational tool and as
a deterministic reward environment for reinforcement-learning poetry
generation, and its integration into PyThaiNLP makes the improvement
available to the entire Thai NLP ecosystem.
