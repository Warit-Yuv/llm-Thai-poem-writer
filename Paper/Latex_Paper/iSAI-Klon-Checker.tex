\begin{abstract}
In Thai \thgloss{Klon-Paed}{กลอนแปด} poetry (the eight-syllable verse form) each four-line stanza must satisfy an interlocking rhyming pattern
where each rhyming syllables must share both a vowel sound \thpar{sara}{สระ} and a final-consonant
class \thpar{mattra}{มาตราตัวสะกด}. Because Thai is written without word
boundaries and its orthography conceals vowels and silenced letters,
automatic rhyme verification is non-trivial: neither classical rule-based
checkers nor G2P-romanization checkers handle the resulting edge cases
reliably. We present a hybrid rule-based verification approach: first, we
improve the KhaveeVerifier algorithm---vowel analysis,
final-consonant-class analysis, the rhyme test, karun silencing, and
true-final detection---and ship it in PyThaiNLP 5.3.5 (Model~B); second,
we introduce Klonpad (Model~D), which augments these rules with a
gold-standard G2P override dictionary of over 4{,}000 entries and a
fallback segmenter, recovering phonetic blind spots of the base oracle. We evaluate
five checker configurations on a gold corpus of 36{,}475 classical stanzas
(145{,}709 rhyme checks) and on a targeted augmentation of 11{,}623
instances including 10{,}000 adversarial negatives and 423 oracle-blind
rhymes. The best configuration reaches 88.5\% gold recall and 86.9\% F1 on
the augmentation, 100\% precision on curated negatives, and 95.7\% of the
oracle-blind rhymes. The verifier is released as an explainable
educational tool and provides a deterministic reward signal for RL-based
Klon-Paed generation.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Thai orthography does not map straightforwardly onto pronunciation, and
this gap becomes especially apparent in the classical register used by Thai
poetry. The script is written without explicit word boundaries; vowels are
frequently implicit; consonants or entire clusters can be silenced by the
karun diacritic \thpar{thanthakhat}{ทัณฑฆาต}; and irregular words whose pronunciations do
not follow the standard phonetic rules are common. Complex phonological
structures, such as consonant blending \thpar{\emph{kham khuap klam}}{คำควบกล้ำ} and leading
consonants \thpar{\emph{aksorn nam}}{อักษรนำ}, further obscure syllable boundaries, vowel
classification, and tone. As a result, syllabification and pronunciation
are particularly difficult for computational algorithms and machine
learning models.

Klon-Paed is a Thai verse form that depends on these
phonetic properties. Each stanza \thpar{\emph{bot}}{บท} consists of four
sets of seven-to-nine-syllable lines \thpar{\emph{wak}}{วรรค}. A valid rhyme
requires two syllables to share both the vowel sound \thpar{sara}{สระ} and
the final-consonant class \thpar{mattra}{มาตราตัวสะกด}. Four canonical rhyme
rules must hold within (r1, r2, r3) and across (rX) stanzas
(Section~\ref{sec:bg}), and the final syllable of every wak must participate.
A single silent letter, e.g.\ the silent \emph{\thpar{r}{ร}} of
\emph{\thgloss{phet}{เพชร}} (`diamond'), or a short-long vowel-length
error is enough to break a rhyme. These constraints are strict and
deterministic, which is difficult for statistical systems.

A reliable automatic verifier is needed in two communities. In
\emph{education}, teachers and students need deterministic, explainable,
hallucination-free feedback: an LLM that invents an incorrect rhyme should
not be used in a classroom. In \emph{NLP research}, training a language
model to compose Klon-Paed, e.g.\ via reinforcement learning (RL),
requires a programmatic reward that scores rhyme correctness. Because
LLMs are probabilistic, they are unreliable at strict constraints such as
syllable counts and rhymes, which a rule-based verifier supplies
deterministically.

Existing verification systems fall short of this standard. The prior
KhaveeVerifier in PyThaiNLP 5.0.1 (Model~A) relies on dictionary subword
tokenization, omits one canonical rhyme rule, and reaches only 73.4\%
recall on our gold corpus. G2P romanization-based checkers, such as the
\texttt{word\_check} grader of KlonSuphap-LM~\cite{klonsuphap} (Model~C),
collapse long/short vowel distinctions and produce thousands of false
positives on adversarial negatives. In this paper we
address these limitations with a hybrid rule-based approach and a
rigorous, adversarial evaluation. Our contributions are:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{An improved rule-based verifier (Model~B).} We rework the
vowel analysis (\texttt{check\_sara}), the final-consonant-class analysis
(\texttt{check\_marttra}), the rhyme test (\texttt{is\_sumpus}), karun
silencing, and true-final detection, and implement all four canonical rhyme
rules. The result is merged into PyThaiNLP 5.3.5 as the default
\texttt{KhaveeVerifier}.
\item \textbf{A dictionary-enhanced variant (Klonpad, Model~D).} We augment
the rules with a gold-standard G2P override dictionary of over 4{,}000
entries and a fallback segmenter, recovering the oracle's blind spots.
\item \textbf{A large evaluation corpus.} 36{,}475 gold stanzas (145{,}709
rhyme checks) from five classical works, plus 11{,}623 augmentation
instances: 10{,}000 oracle-verified negatives, 1{,}200 tricky positives,
and 423 oracle-blind positives.
\item \textbf{A systematic comparison.} Five configurations (A, B, C,
D\_w2p, D\_ssg) are compared on precision, recall, F1, and coverage, with
per-rule and per-operator error analyses.
\item \textbf{Educational tool and RL-ready reward.} An explainable web
tool plus a deterministic reward signal for RL-based Klon-Paed generation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Background and Related Work}
\label{sec:bg}

\subsection{The Linguistic Rules of Klon-Paed}

A Klon-Paed stanza has four waks of eight syllables each. Thai syllables
are written without delimiters and a word may consist of one or more
syllables, so the meter must be recovered by syllabification rather than by
counting words. Within this skeleton the verse follows a fixed rhyme scheme.
As defined by the Royal Institute of Thailand, two syllables rhyme \thpar{\emph{sumpus}}{สัมผัส}
when they share the same vowel sound \thpar{sara}{สระ} and the same final-consonant class
\thpar{mattra}{มาตราตัวสะกด}. Additionally, tone is not part of the rhyme.
The mattra classes \thpar{\emph{mae}}{แม่}, consisting of ka, kong, kok,
kot, kon, kop, kom, koey, and koew, group the final consonants of Thai
syllables by their phonetic outcome (the velar, dental, labial, nasal,
semivowel, and \thpar{\emph{w}}{ว} finals). The four waks are called the
\thpar{\emph{sadap}}{สดับ},
\thpar{\emph{rap}}{รับ}, \thpar{\emph{rong}}{รอง}, and
\thpar{\emph{song}}{ส่ง} respectively. Four canonical
rhyme rules are checked in this work, following the \emph{klon} canon and
the implementation of the PyThaiNLP \texttt{KhaveeVerifier}:
\begin{itemize}
\item r1 (\emph{sadap} $\rightarrow$ \emph{rap}): the final syllable of
wak~1 must rhyme with one of the first five syllables of wak~2 (syllables
3 and 5 are obligatory, 1, 2, and 4 are permissible);
\item r2 (\emph{rap} $\rightarrow$ \emph{rong}): the final syllable of
wak~2 must rhyme with the final syllable of wak~3;
\item r3 (\emph{rong} $\rightarrow$ \emph{song}): the final syllable of
wak~3 must rhyme with one of the first five syllables of wak~4 (syllables
3 and 5 are obligatory, 1, 2, and 4 are permissible);
\item rX (inter-stanza): the final syllable of wak~4 of the previous
stanza rhymes with the final syllable of wak~2 of the current stanza.
\end{itemize}
As a concrete example, the well-known stanza of the
\thgloss{\emph{Phukao Thong}}{นิราศภูเขาทอง} travel poem by Sunthorn Phu
satisfies r1--r3; Figure~\ref{fig:klon} shows its rhyme positions and the
inter-stanza (rX) link to the preceding stanza.

Three orthographic phenomena make these rules hard to apply automatically.
First, compound, transformed, and reduced vowels \thpar{\emph{sara prasom}}{สระประสม},
\thpar{\emph{sara plianrup}}{สระเปลี่ยนรูป}, \thpar{\emph{sara lotrup}}{สระลดรูป} let several spellings map to one
vowel sound. Second, the karun diacritic silences a letter
or a whole cluster, leaving unpronounced letters in the written form.
Third, a trailing \thgloss{y, l, w}{ย, ล, ว} may be a genuine final
consonant or merely part of a vowel digraph or an initial cluster. Any
verifier must resolve all three before it can compare vowel and final
class.

\subsection{Thai Text-Processing Foundations}

Two building blocks underlie all Thai NLP systems: segmentation and
grapheme-to-phoneme conversion. Because Thai has no word delimiters,
segmentation has received sustained attention: dictionary-based maximum
matching (the \texttt{newmm} engine of PyThaiNLP~\cite{pythainlp}),
neural segmenters such as DeepCut~\cite{deepcut} and
AttaCut~\cite{attacut}, syllable-based neural
segmentation~\cite{chormai2020syllable}, and the SSG syllable segmenter
shipped in PyThaiNLP~5.x, used throughout this work. G2P conversion maps
surface orthography to pronunciation. Thai G2P has been addressed with
rule-based (TLTK~\cite{tltk}), example-based~\cite{charoenpornsawat2006},
CRF joint-sequence~\cite{saychum2016}, and two-stage
sequence~\cite{rugchatjaroen2019} models. G2P is directly relevant to rhyme
verification because rhyme is a property of \emph{sound}, not of spelling.

\subsection{Computational Poetry Analysis and Generation}

Prior work on Thai poetry in NLP has concentrated on analysis and
generation. On the analysis side, machine-learning studies of Sunthorn
Phu's verse have extracted sound patterns such as rhyme, assonance, and
alliteration using internal-rhyme rules~\cite{suksanguan2021}. On the
generation side, the dominant line of work fine-tunes language models on
corpora of classical verse: the Phra Aphai Mani LM~\cite{phraaphai} and its
successor KlonSuphap-LM~\cite{klonsuphap} fine-tune a GPT-2 Thai base
model, then use \emph{rhyme tagging} (annotating the tokens that must
rhyme), attention masking, and finally reinforcement learning in which a
rule-based \emph{klon grader} supplies the reward. That grader is the
\texttt{word\_check} module evaluated here as Model~C, making our
evaluation directly relevant to the RL step of that pipeline. More
broadly, RLHF has established reward models as essential for aligning LLMs
to follow strict requirements~\cite{ouyang2022}, and grammar-constrained decoding
enforces structural constraints at inference time~\cite{geng2023}; recent
benchmarks show that even strong LLMs struggle with classical Thai
vocabulary and verse~\cite{triyason2026}, motivating deterministic tools.

\subsection{Positioning of This Work}

Compared with this landscape, our contribution is the first systematic,
adversarially augmented evaluation of Thai rhyme \emph{verification} as
opposed to generation: Model~A has never been stress-tested against
curated edge cases, the G2P-based grader (Model~C) has not been validated
for precision, and no open verifier covers all four rhyme rules with
dictionary support for irregular pronunciations. We fill this gap with
improved rule-based verification (Model~B), a dictionary-enhanced variant
(Model~D), and a large gold-with-augmentation evaluation, including a
novel \emph{oracle-blind} probe that holds the verifier accountable
for rhymes its oracle cannot see.

\section{Methodology: Hybrid Verification Architecture}

\subsection{Core Rule-Based Verification (Model B)}

Model~B is the \texttt{KhaveeVerifier} of PyThaiNLP 5.3.5. It verifies a
poem in three stages. (1)~Each wak is tokenised directly into syllables
with the SSG CRF segmenter, applied to the wak text itself without word
pre-segmentation. (2)~For each canonical rule the relevant syllables are
extracted: the final syllable of every wak and the first five syllables of
waks~2 and~4. (3)~The rhyme test \texttt{is\_sumpus} decides whether two
syllables rhyme; a rule holds if the test succeeds for at least one
permitted target. The eight-syllable meter is additionally reported.
Algorithm~\ref{alg:klonalg} summarises this flow. The \texttt{check\_klon}
entry point splits the poem on whitespace, groups waks into stanzas of
exactly four (rejecting incomplete stanzas), enforces the metre with
per-wak word-count limits (ten for Klon~8, five for Klon~4), and reports
each violation as a structured message naming the stanza, wak, and
offending syllables that failed to rhyme.

\begin{algorithm}[t]
\caption{Rhyme verification of a Klon-Paed poem (\texttt{check\_klon}).}
\label{alg:klonalg}
\begin{algorithmic}[1]
\REQUIRE poem $T$ with waks separated by whitespace; type $k\in\{4,8\}$
\ENSURE list of rhyme errors (empty $\Leftrightarrow$ poem is valid)
\STATE $W\gets$ split $T$ into waks, grouped into stanzas of four
\FOR{each stanza $(w_1,w_2,w_3,w_4)$}
  \STATE $S_i\gets$ \textsc{ssg}($w_i$), $i=1,\ldots,4$ \COMMENT{syllables}
  \FOR{each rule $\rho\in\{\mathrm{r1},\mathrm{r2},\mathrm{r3},\mathrm{rX}\}$}
    \STATE $(a,B_\rho)\gets$ source syllable and target set of rule $\rho$
    \IF{no $b\in B_\rho$ satisfies \textsc{is\_sumpus}($a,b$)}
      \STATE record an error for rule $\rho$
    \ENDIF
  \ENDFOR
\ENDFOR
\RETURN the list of errors
\end{algorithmic}
\end{algorithm}

The accuracy of the verifier rests on three functions.
\texttt{check\_sara} returns the canonical vowel of a syllable. Its
per-character loop was rewritten to remove the shadowed conditions that
misparsed the \emph{ua} diphthong and to resolve the transformed and
reduced vowels signalled by the short-vowel mark
\thpar{\emph{mai tai khu}}{ไม้ไต่คู้}: \emph{cet} $\rightarrow$ short
\emph{e}, \emph{khaeng} $\rightarrow$ short \emph{ae}, \emph{ko}
$\rightarrow$ \thpar{\emph{ao}}{เอาะ}. The
\thgloss{\emph{ro han}}{รร} is evaluated once, yielding \emph{am} in a
\emph{mae kom} context (e.g., \thgloss{\emph{tham}}{ธรรม}) and \emph{a}
elsewhere. The three readings of \thpar{\emph{ru}}{ฤ} and
\thpar{\emph{lue}}{ฦ} are classified from the original spelling
\emph{before} karun stripping, so silent suffixes cannot hide them:
\emph{roek} in \thgloss{\emph{roek}}{ฤกษ์}, \emph{rit} in
\thgloss{\emph{rit}}{ฤทธิ์} and \thgloss{\emph{angkit}}{อังกฤษ}, and
\emph{rue-du} in \thgloss{\emph{ruedu}}{ฤดู}. Pali/Sanskrit words with a
silent terminal vowel (e.g., \thgloss{\emph{kiet}}{เกียรติ},
\thgloss{\emph{chat}}{ชาติ}, \thgloss{\emph{thammachat}}{ธรรมชาติ}) have it
stripped, and an empty result falls back to the implied short \emph{a}.
\texttt{check\_marttra} returns the Royal-Institute final-consonant class
of a syllable, strictly by orthography. It resolves karun first, strips
silent terminal vowels, then strips a silent \emph{r} in loanword
clusters---always in \emph{tr}/\emph{chr}/\emph{pr} (e.g., \emph{but},
\emph{net}, \thgloss{\emph{phet}}{เพชร}) but in \emph{kr}, \emph{kh-r},
and \emph{th-r} only when the preceding vowel is short, so
\thgloss{\emph{cak}}{จักร} loses its \emph{r} while
\thgloss{\emph{mangkon}}{มังกร} does not. Standalone characters
(\thgloss{\emph{na}}{ณ}, \thgloss{\emph{tha}}{ธ},
\thgloss{\emph{phona}}{พณ}, \thgloss{\emph{bo}}{บ}) and the \emph{sara
koen} are classed as \emph{mae ka}. Finally, \texttt{is\_sumpus}
normalizes both arguments independently, since the old conditional chain
short-circuited when only one word needed normalization:
\emph{a+koey} $\rightarrow$ \emph{ai+ka} (so \emph{chai} rhymes with
\emph{sai}) and \emph{a/am+kom} $\rightarrow$ \emph{am+ka} (so \emph{cam}
rhymes with \emph{kam}); it returns true iff the normalised sara and
mattra of the two syllables are equal.

Two further mechanisms account for most of the improvement over the 5.0.1
baseline. \texttt{handle\_karun\_sound\_silence} strips characters silenced
by the karun, no longer by a fixed truncation: it recognises
multi-letter silent suffixes such as \thgloss{\emph{phra lak}}{พระลักษมณ์}
(strips the silent \emph{km} cluster to expose the true \emph{mae kok}
final) and \thgloss{\emph{kasat}}{กษัตริย์} (strips the silent \emph{triy}
suffix, leaving a \emph{mae kot} final), two-consonant suffixes such as
\emph{-tr} in \thgloss{\emph{sat}}{ศาสตร์} and \emph{-nthr} in
\thgloss{\emph{can}}{จันทร์}, and the consonant-plus-vowel patterns of
\thgloss{\emph{sit}}{สิทธิ์} and \thgloss{\emph{phan}}{พันธุ์}, while leaving
an internal karun (e.g., \thgloss{\emph{ohm}}{โอห์ม}) untouched.
\texttt{\_is\_true\_final} decides whether a trailing \emph{y}, \emph{l},
or \emph{w} is a genuine final rather than part of a vowel digraph or
initial cluster. It strips karun and tone marks first---so
\thgloss{\emph{klai}}{ใกล้}, which ends in a tone mark, is still seen as
ending in \emph{l}---requires at least two consonants, and consults
hand-built cluster tables for \emph{l}, \emph{r}, and \emph{w} under the
pre-posed vowels \thpar{\emph{e-}, \emph{ae-}, \emph{o-}, \emph{ai-}}{เ- แ- โ- ไ- ใ-}:
the \emph{y} of \thgloss{\emph{thai}}{ไทย} is silent, the \emph{l} of
\thgloss{\emph{plae}}{เปล} belongs to the cluster, and the \emph{l} of
\thgloss{\emph{chon}}{ชล} is a true final.

\subsection{Dictionary-Enhanced Verification (Klonpad, Model D)}

Model~B is linguistically principled but inherits a blind spot from its own
design: \texttt{check\_sara} returns only the first vowel of a token, so a
multi-syllable word that SSG keeps as one token loses the vowel of its
final (rhyming) syllable, and silent-\emph{r} loanwords are stripped only
in a whitelisted subset of clusters. Klonpad (Model~D) addresses this by
layering a gold-standard pronunciation dictionary on top of Model~B's
rules.

The dictionary, \texttt{POETRY\_OVERRIDES}, contains over 4{,}000
hand-verified entries mapping a surface form to its syllable sequence (e.g.,
\thgloss{\emph{phet}}{เพชร} $\rightarrow$ \emph{phet}; \thgloss{\emph{sattruu}}{ศัตรู} $\rightarrow$
\emph{sat-truu}; \thgloss{\emph{kasatrii}}{กษัตรี} $\rightarrow$ \emph{ka-sat-trii}). It was
built from the frequent vocabulary of the Phra Aphai Mani corpus with
manual supervision, and every entry is checked by a four-guard pipeline
that rejects pronunciations hallucinated by the Word2Phrase (w2p) neural
G2P model; w2p-derived candidates are kept only as labelled hints and never
trusted on their own. At inference time Klonpad segments the wak into
words with an override-augmented newmm dictionary, looks each up in the
override dictionary, and syllabifies unmatched words with the fallback
segmenter. We report two configurations: \texttt{D\_w2p}, which falls back
to the w2p neural G2P model, and \texttt{D\_ssg}, which falls back to
plain SSG segmentation; as Section~\ref{sec:results} shows, the w2p
fallback hallucinates on short or compound words, making \texttt{D\_ssg}
the more robust configuration.

\subsection{Baseline Systems}

Model~A is the original \texttt{KhaveeVerifier} of PyThaiNLP 5.0.1,
instrumented verbatim from the vendored source: dictionary-based
\texttt{subword\_tokenize}, no r3 rule, no karun handling, no true-final
logic. Model~C is the \texttt{word\_check} rhyme grader of
KlonSuphap-LM~\cite{klonsuphap}: each wak is romanized with the TLTK G2P
model and the romanized vowels and finals compared. Its two documented
weaknesses are (i)~a \texttt{replace\_long\_short} step that collapses
long and short vowels and (ii)~a mattra comparison that ignores the final
consonant, both inflating recall at the cost of precision; tltk failures
reduce coverage.

\section{Dataset Construction and Augmentation}

\subsection{Gold-Standard Corpus Compilation}

We collected five classical Thai narrative poems from the Vajirayana
digital library~\cite{vajirayana}: \thgloss{\emph{Khobut}}{โคบุตร},
\thgloss{\emph{Khun Chang Khun Phaen}}{ขุนช้างขุนแผน}, \thgloss{\emph{Phra Aphai Mani}}{พระอภัยมณี},
\thgloss{\emph{Phukao Tong}}{ภูเขาทอง}, and \thgloss{\emph{Suphasaet Son
Ying}}{สุภาษิตสอนหญิง}. A cleaning pipeline normalised the text and split it
into stanzas of four waks, discarding only chapter-opening fragments and
scribal \emph{o} marks. Crucially, waks with ten or more syllables were
retained: dropping them severs inter-stanza rhyme chains and made an early
corpus version appear to violate rX at a ~72\% rate, whereas on the
complete corpus rX holds at ~96--97\%. The final corpus comprises 191
files, 36{,}475 stanzas, 145{,}900 waks, and 145{,}709 gold rhyme checks;
the gold is all-positive, because the classical poems are presumed to
rhyme. The Thai Literature Corpora~\cite{tlc} cover similar material.

\subsection{Targeted Data Augmentation}

Recall over an all-positive corpus cannot measure precision, and easy
positives cannot expose blind spots. We therefore augmented the gold with
adversarial instances generated by an automated pipeline. Every instance
is \emph{standalone}: it is derived from a real stanza but only the target
rule is changed, so exactly one rule is exercised while the others remain
intact. Every negative is verified by the 5.3.5 oracle
(\texttt{is\_sumpus}) to be a genuine non-rhyme, and the whole set was
audited in four independent passes in which reviewers spelled out the sara
and mattra of every candidate (with vowel length made explicit) and
double-checked pronunciations against the IPA. The audit removed 56
candidate words the oracle misreads---karun clusters it fails to silence
and multi-syllable Pali words SSG keeps as single tokens.

\paragraph{Negatives (precision probes).} The 10{,}000 negatives are drawn
from eight corruption operators: trivial different-vowel-and-mattra swaps
(C6, 300), same-mattra swaps (C0, 500), same-vowel swaps (C1, 500),
short/long vowel swaps (C3, 3{,}100), liquid-final swaps (C4, 1{,}200), a
systematic trap in which the 5.0.1 checker reads the candidate as rhyming
with the target while 5.3.5 does not (C9, 2{,}800), leading-consonant words
(C5, 600), and karun/\thgloss{\emph{ru}}{ฤ}/cluster traps (C2, 1{,}000).
C3 and C9 dominate the mix because they are the operators that best
discriminate the checkers.

\paragraph{Positives (recall probes).} The 1{,}200 \emph{tricky}
positives swap the target syllable with a rhyming candidate that exercises
normalization \thpar{\emph{sara plianrup/lotrup}}{สระเปลี่ยนรูป/ลดรูป}, \thgloss{\emph{ru}}{ฤ}, karun, and
true-final cases; all are oracle-confirmed rhymes. A further 423
\emph{oracle-blind} positives are genuine rhymes the oracle labels as
non-rhymes: the silent-\emph{r} \thgloss{\emph{phet}}{เพชร} (true
\emph{eh}+kot, which the oracle reads with the long \emph{ae} because the
dead final carries no short-vowel mark), and the first-sara words
\thgloss{\emph{sattruu}}{ศัตรู}, \thgloss{\emph{kasatrii}}{กษัตรี}, and
\thgloss{\emph{kasatriiya}}{กษัตรีย์} (SSG keeps each as one token, so
\texttt{check\_sara} hears only the first vowel). For these the gold label
is the \emph{linguistic} truth, not the oracle verdict: a checker that
hears the real rhyme receives credit, while the oracle itself (Model~B) is
charged a false negative---``the oracle that is wrong loses points.''

\section{Evaluation and Results}
\label{sec:results}

\subsection{Experimental Setup}

We report precision, recall, and F1 with Wilson 95\% confidence intervals,
coverage, and a conservative drop-as-fail variant for Model~C. Because
every augmentation instance is standalone and tests exactly one rule, the
per-rule prediction is the clean metric; a stanza-level aggregate over the
augmentation is only well-defined for Model~B (the oracle). Model~A has no
r3 and is reported as N/A there. All checkers were instrumented from
verbatim vendored sources (the 5.0.1 and 5.3.5 cores), with Model~C run
under Python 3.12 through a persistent pool of ten tltk workers (the full
augmentation pass takes about 40 minutes; A/B/D are parallelised and finish
in under a minute).

\subsection{Discussion}

% น่าใส่เรื่อง G2P dict https://github.com/PyThaiNLP/thai-g2p-wiktionary-corpus
% กับเรื่องคำเดี๋ยวกันแต่มันอ่านได้หลายแบบ (heteronyms?) แล้วก็พยางค์ด้วยว่าตอนเราออกเสียง
% บางทีก็สามารถรวบคำให้มันลงสัมผัสได้ ซึ่ง dictionary overrides ตอนนี้เรา force ให้มันเป็นแบบเดียวกันหมด

The experiment supports four findings. (1)~\textbf{The proposed rules are
precise.} B never false-accepts on oracle-verified negatives, and B,
D\_w2p, and D\_ssg all exceed 99.5\% merged precision (A 95\%, C 92\%).
(2)~\textbf{The override dictionary recovers the oracle's blind spots.}
The D\_w2p-versus-D\_ssg gap on oracle-blind recall (95.7\% versus 68.1\%)
is the pronunciation-knowledge contribution: the overrides read
\emph{phet} as /phet/ and split \emph{sattruu} and \emph{kasatrii} into
their true syllables. One limitation is that each entry forces a single
canonical pronunciation, although many surface forms are heteronyms whose
reading depends on context, and in natural delivery adjacent syllables are
often merged so that a rhyme lands. The current entries are handcrafted:
they originate from Word2Phrase (w2p) candidate readings that were
verified manually, and no public G2P corpus has been used yet. Mining a
gold-standard G2P corpus to expand the dictionary is future work to
improve coverage and accuracy. (3)~\textbf{C trades precision for recall
on exactly
the vowel-length distinction} the rule-based family enforces (78.7\%
oracle-blind recall, but 30.4\% precision). (4)~\textbf{The oracle itself has a blind spot} that
only the oracle-blind probe exposes: a purely oracle-supervised evaluation
would report B as flawless. On the merged gold+augmentation set, F1
favours D\_ssg (93.4\%), D\_w2p (92.9\%), and B (92.8\%), with C (88.0\%)
and A (82.3\%) behind. The discriminating precision lives in the
augmentation-only tables.

\section{Applications and Future Work}

\subsection{Educational Impact}

The verifier is deployed as an interactive web tool for Klon 4/8 teachers
and students: it segments a poem into syllables, highlights rhyme
positions, spells out the sara and mattra of every syllable, and explains
each error in terms of the four rhyme rules. The rule-based
feedback is reproducible and hallucination-free. Future work includes
classroom evaluations and pronunciation guidance for irregular words.

\subsection{Foundation for Generative AI}

The same determinism makes the verifier a suitable reward signal for
fine-tuning small, locally run LLMs: exact, immediate, and free of the
noise of a learned reward model. It mirrors the RL step of the
KlonSuphap-LM pipeline~\cite{klonsuphap}, but with a grader (Model~B or~D)
substantially more precise than the G2P-based grader used there (Model~C),
particularly on vowel length. Fine-tuning a generator with this reward,
scaling the override dictionary from a public gold-standard G2P corpus, and
handling heteronyms and on-the-fly syllable merging are future work,
alongside decoding-time constrained sampling~\cite{geng2023}.

\section{Conclusion}

We presented a hybrid rule-based approach to Thai Klon-Paed rhyme
verification. An improved rule-based verifier (Model~B), now the default
\texttt{KhaveeVerifier} of PyThaiNLP 5.3.5, implements all four canonical
rhyme rules with robust karun, true-final, and sara handling and reaches
100\% precision on curated negatives. A dictionary-enhanced variant
(Klonpad, Model~D) adds a gold-standard G2P override dictionary of over
4{,}000 entries and recovers 95.7\% of the oracle's own blind spots. On
36{,}475 gold stanzas and 11{,}623 adversarial instances, the proposed
family raises stanza recall from 73.4\% to 88.5\% (D\_ssg) with
near-perfect precision, exposing the blind spots of both the
G2P-romanization checker and the rule-based oracle. The verifier is
released as an explainable educational tool and provides a deterministic
reward signal for RL-based generation; its integration into PyThaiNLP
makes the improvement available to the entire Thai NLP ecosystem.
